is buying a highly anticipated IPO on its first trading day a good long-term investment?

Taken together, these separate observational findings suggest that first-day buying risk looks greatest for heavily hyped US IPOs; they show association, not that hype causes later weakness.

in short

  • C13Interpretation: The first-day-buyer risk looks greatest for heavily hyped IPOs. Young firms, busy-market issues, high pre-IPO social-media enthusiasm and extra-hot first-day jumps are each linked to weaker later returns. These are separate, observational findings from different periods and horizons, and they show association, not that hype causes the later weakness.
    “IPOs with strong early excitement average a 29.73% initial return, but suffer a − 8.22% long-term industry-adjusted return”
    Social Media Emotions and IPO Returns · Journal of money credit and banking · 2024

age and market activity

  • C2Ritter found the underperformance was not uniform: it was worst for young firms and for IPOs in high-volume years, and absent for older firms and in light-volume years. This is relevant because heavily hyped IPOs are likely to be young and issued in busy markets.
    “Younger companies and companies going public in heavy volume years did even worse than average.”
    The Long‐Run Performance of initial Public Offerings · The Journal of Finance · 1991

early enthusiasm and later returns

  • C7Pre-IPO social-media enthusiasm is associated with higher first-day returns but lower later industry-adjusted returns: IPOs with high enthusiasm averaged a 29.73% first-day return and a -8.22% long-run industry-adjusted return.
    “IPOs with strong early excitement average a 29.73% initial return, but suffer a − 8.22% long-term industry-adjusted return”
    Social Media Emotions and IPO Returns · Journal of money credit and banking · 2024
